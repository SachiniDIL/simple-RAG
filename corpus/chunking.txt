What is chunking and why does it matter?

Before you can embed a document, you usually need to split it into smaller pieces called
chunks. This matters for two reasons. First, embedding models have a maximum input length,
so a whole book cannot be embedded as a single vector. Second, and more importantly, a single
embedding for an entire long document tends to blur together many different topics, which
makes retrieval worse. If a document covers ten different subjects and you embed the whole
thing as one vector, that vector represents a vague average of all ten subjects rather than
any one of them clearly.

A common strategy is to split text into chunks of a few hundred words, often with some
overlap between consecutive chunks so that a sentence sitting right on a chunk boundary is
not cut in half and orphaned from its context. Choosing chunk size is a tradeoff: chunks that
are too small lose surrounding context and can be misleading in isolation, while chunks that
are too large dilute the specific meaning that made them relevant to a query in the first
place. There is no universally correct chunk size; it depends on the type of document and how
questions will be asked against it.
